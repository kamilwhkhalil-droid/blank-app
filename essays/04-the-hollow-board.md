# The Hollow Board

*Board Reading No. 4 in a six-part series on how organizations die.*

---

An organization's license to operate is not one number. It is four, and the lowest one binds.

**Consumer legitimacy:** do customers believe you deliver value?
**Market legitimacy:** do investors and partners believe you are viable?
**Regulatory legitimacy:** do governments permit you to operate?
**Internal legitimacy:** do your own people believe in the mission?

Average them and you get a comfortable middle that hides the only number that matters. Score them separately and a specific death pattern becomes visible: **the first three strong, the fourth sliding.** External reputation intact — customers loyal, investors warm, regulators cooperative — while the people closest to the work are quietly repricing the company.

That is the Hollow Board, and it is not a morale problem. It is a forecast.

Your employees hold information no outside party has: what the product roadmap actually looks like from inside, which promises are being kept, what the gap is between the story told upstairs and the work done downstairs. When they start leaving — or worse, staying and disengaging — they are not reacting to the collapse. They are *preceding* it. The people closest to the work reprice you before any outside party does. They always do. An organization admired by everyone except its own employees is showing you the order of the coming collapse, not its absence.

The pattern predicted the internal-sentiment collapses that preceded WeWork, Boeing, and Credit Suisse — in each case, external channels held while internal belief drained, and in each case the external channels followed. Legitimacy channels are not independent; a failure that looks contained in one channel is a delayed failure in all four. Abraaj Group, a fourteen-billion-dollar private equity firm, scored high on every visible channel in 2017. One investor asked one question about where the money had gone. Within four months, every channel collapsed in sequence.

Two practices follow:

1. **Never average the four channels.** The lowest channel is the binding constraint. Report all four, and treat the weakest as the headline.
2. **Treat high-performer attrition as a leading indicator, not an HR metric.** The people with the most options and the most information leave first. That ordering is the signal.

If your Glassdoor is diverging from your press coverage, believe your Glassdoor. The press is reporting your past. Your employees are reporting your future.
