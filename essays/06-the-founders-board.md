# The Founder's Board

*Board Reading No. 6 — the last in a series on how organizations die. This one is mine.*

---

Five readings in this series diagnosed famous corpses: Nokia, GM, Lehman, the hollowed-out giants, Quibi. The sixth is different. It is the most common reading in the world, it has never made a headline, and the odds are that if you run a small company, you are looking at it right now.

I know, because I was.

**Stable Potential. Faintly negative Velocity. Thin Buffer. Mid Floor.**

Here is what that looked like from inside my own company — a services firm in Beirut, which is to say a company running its survival experiment inside a country running one too. We had one client that was the best thing that had ever happened to us: reliable, engaged, professional. That client was roughly forty percent of revenue. And I remember sitting with a new request from them — a good request, more work, more money — and feeling something I could only later name: the feeling of being offered more of the thing that was going to kill us.

The dashboard, when I finally scored it: Potential 6 — stable, but it was the stillness of a company being quietly shaped into one client's custom shop. Velocity −0.5 — small, negative, and easy to explain away, which is exactly how negative Velocity kills you: never large enough, in any single quarter, to force a response. Buffer 4 — enough to feel safe on a good week, not enough to survive the loss of a forty-percent client. Floor 5 — the one piece of good news: we were slipping, but not yet trapped.

Nothing on that board forces action. Everything on it permits decline. That is the Founder's Board: the reading of a company that will be fine right up until the quarter it very suddenly is not.

What makes it survivable is the one variable founders never look at: the Floor. Ours was low enough to still permit movement — we could still change shape without triggering collapse. That single fact, which I did not appreciate at the time, is the reason the story has a happy ending. We capped the client's share of revenue. We said no to good money. It felt, for months, like losing our nerve. It was the opposite.

Two numbers to score this week, honestly, before your next good news arrives:

1. **Your largest client's share of revenue.** If it is above 25% and rising, the trap is arming itself — and it will feel like success the entire time.
2. **Your Velocity, with the sign checked twice.** If it is faintly negative and you have a reasonable explanation, remember that reasonable explanations are what quiet deaths are made of.

The board cannot make the decision for you. It can only make the decision *arguable* — a specific claim with evidence, instead of a feeling that things are probably fine. That turns out to be the whole difference.
