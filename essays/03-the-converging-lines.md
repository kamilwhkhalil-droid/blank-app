# The Converging Lines

*Board Reading No. 3 in a six-part series on how organizations die.*

---

Lehman Brothers did not fail on September 15, 2008. It failed over the seven prior years, with mechanical precision, on a schedule set by two lines nobody graphed.

**Line one: Buffer, falling.** From 2001 onward, Lehman spent its absorptive capacity — acquiring five mortgage originators on the assumption that national housing prices would never fall, an assumption with no historical basis, waved through as *this time is different*. By 2007 its leverage ratio was 30.8 to 1. A 3.3% decline in asset value would erase the firm.

**Line two: Floor, rising.** In the same years, Lehman accumulated obligations — an $85 billion mortgage book, funding structures that demanded continuous market confidence. Its own chief risk officer put the warning in writing in 2006. The warning was ignored.

That is the reading: **Buffer falling while the Floor rises to meet it.** Spending your shock absorber in the exact years you are accumulating the obligations that will demand it.

The lethal property of this reading is that neither line alarms anyone in any single quarter. Each quarter's leverage is only slightly worse than the last. Each new obligation is individually rational. The lines converge slowly, and the intersection is scheduled years in advance by the slopes. At the point where they meet, a formality occurs.

The subprime market fell 5%. Lehman — which had survived the Civil War, the Depression, and two World Wars — vaporized. Bear Stearns had collapsed six months earlier, and Lehman watched, held its leverage, and explained it away: *Bear was poorly managed. We are different.* By summer it reported its first quarterly loss in fourteen years, raised $6 billion, and changed nothing about the business model — processing a solvency problem as a liquidity problem. Seven years of signal. None of it metabolized.

The defense is embarrassingly simple, which is why almost nobody mounts it: **graph the two lines together.** Not in the same board pack, on the same chart. Your buffer trajectory and your obligation trajectory, quarter over quarter. If the slopes converge, you do not have a bad quarter coming. You have an intersection scheduled — and the only question the chart cannot answer is whether you will renegotiate the slopes before the date the slopes have already picked.
